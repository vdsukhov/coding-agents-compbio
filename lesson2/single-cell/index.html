Lesson 2 · Single-cell primer · pbmc3k, 2,638 blood cells

From counts to clusters

A single-cell experiment starts as a sparse table of counts. Nine steps turn it into groups of similar cells and the genes that define them. Every figure here is drawn from a real analysis of the pbmc3k dataset.

01Input

From cells to a count matrix

Droplet methods (Drop-seq, inDrop, and 10x Chromium, which produced pbmc3k) pair single cells with barcoded beads inside tiny oil droplets. Every primer on a bead carries the same cell barcode plus a random UMI. When the cell bursts, its mRNA sticks to the bead, so each captured molecule is tagged with both: which droplet it came from, and which molecule it was.

Cells arrive at random, so the number per droplet follows a Poisson distribution. Most droplets get no cell; a few get two, a doublet that looks like one cell with a mixed profile.

Toy model of droplet encapsulation

Real runs load few cells (λ near 0.1) so doublets stay rare. The price: most droplets hold only a bead, and their barcodes are filtered out later as empty.

After sequencing, reads are grouped by barcode and UMI into a count matrix: cells × genes, each entry the number of distinct molecules. Only a small fraction of each cell's mRNA is captured, so most entries are zero. A zero means "not observed", not "not expressed".

02Quality control
Remove broken droplets

  

    
Three per-cell metrics catch most problems:

    
    
      
  • Genes detected: too few means an empty droplet or dead cell; too many suggests a doublet (two cells sharing one barcode, as in the step 01 animation).
    • 
      
  • Total UMIs: tracks genes detected closely.
    • 
      
  • Mitochondrial %: dying cells leak cytoplasmic mRNA but keep mitochondrial transcripts, so their mito share rises.
    • 
    

    
The standard pbmc3k cut keeps 200–2,500 genes and <5% mito.

03Normalization
Remove library-size effects

  

    
Droplets differ in how many molecules they capture. Without correction, a well-captured cell looks like it expresses everything more. Log-normalization divides by the cell's total, scales to 10,000, and takes the log so a few very abundant genes do not dominate.

04Feature selection
Keep the genes that vary

  

    
Most genes are barely detected or constant across cells; they add noise. We keep the highly variable genes (HVGs). Because variance grows with mean expression, each gene is judged against genes of similar abundance.

    
vst (Seurat v3+, used here): fit the expected variance for each mean from raw counts, standardize each gene by it, and take the variance of the result. 1 means "as variable as expected"; it can't go below 0. Keep the top 2,000.

    
Dispersion z-score (Seurat v2, older tutorials): z-score each gene's dispersion within bins of similar mean. Negative means less variable than its peers. Same idea, different scale, hence the different shape.

05Scaling & PCA
Compress 2,000 genes into a few axes

  

    
Each gene is scaled to mean 0 and variance 1 (clipped at 10), so highly expressed genes don't dominate. PCA then finds the directions of greatest variation: genes that rise and fall together load onto the same component, and a PC's top-loading genes tell you what it captures.

    
The elbow plot shows where extra PCs stop adding much. We keep the first 10, as in the Seurat tutorial.

06Neighbor graph
Connect each cell to its look-alikes

  

    
For each cell, find the k most similar cells by distance in the 10-PC space, and link them. The resulting kNN graph is the foundation for what follows: both the 2D layout (step 07) and clustering (step 08) are computed from it. Small k keeps fine structure but fragments the graph; large k can merge small groups. We use 15, the scanpy default.

07Visualization
Lay the graph out in 2D with UMAP

  

    
UMAP draws the graph from step 06 so that connected cells sit close together. It is for looking only; no later step uses its coordinates.

    
Start (epoch 0): a spectral layout of the graph, from the two leading eigenvectors of its Laplacian; it already roughly groups well-connected cells. Optimization: for 500 epochs, cells are pulled toward graph neighbors and pushed away from random non-neighbors. Neighborhoods on a UMAP are meaningful; distances between islands and their sizes are not.

08Clustering
Find communities in the graph

  

    
The Leiden algorithm splits the kNN graph into communities: groups with many edges inside, few between. It works on the graph, never on UMAP coordinates; the UMAP is just a convenient place to show the result.

    
Resolution controls granularity. There is no correct value; choose one whose clusters you can explain. At 0.5 (the Seurat tutorial's value) we get 9 clusters.

09Marker genes
Find what makes each cluster different

  

    
For each cluster, every gene is compared against all other cells (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) and ranked by how strongly it is up in that cluster. In the dot plot, dot size is the fraction of cells expressing the gene and color is mean expression; a good marker is large and dark in one row only.

    
The p-values are inflated because the clusters were defined from the same data. Use the ranking, not the p-value.

  Open question
  
What are these nine clusters?

  

    

      
The analysis has found nine groups of cells and the genes that set each one apart. It has not told us what the groups are. Naming them is the next step, annotation, and it needs knowledge from outside this dataset.

      
The most direct approach uses the marker genes from step 09. Decades of immunology tie specific genes to specific cell types: surface proteins used to sort cells by flow cytometry, cytotoxic enzymes, antigen-presentation machinery. If a cluster's top markers match a known signature, that is strong evidence for its identity.

      
Reference-based tools such as CellTypist, Azimuth or SingleR automate the same comparison against large labelled atlases. Their output should still be checked against the markers.

    

    

      
Questions to bring to the marker list:

      
    
        
  • Which clusters share markers, and which have unique ones?
    • 
        
  • Do the top genes appear in published signatures for blood cell types?
    • 
        
  • Are the cluster sizes plausible for blood?
    • 
        
  • Is a cluster defined by real markers, or only by stress, mitochondrial or ribosomal genes? (That can signal low-quality cells or doublets.)
    • 
      

      
Try it with the Well-known immune genes view in step 09 and decide for yourself.

The whole pipeline

  

    
About fifteen lines of code. Every default here is a choice (QC cutoffs, number of HVGs and PCs, k, resolution). Good practice is to vary them and keep the conclusions that survive. Common next steps: doublet detection (Scrublet, scDblFinder), ambient RNA removal (SoupX, CellBender), and batch integration across samples (Harmony, scVI). The Single-cell Best Practices book covers each in depth.

  

  
Full analysis

  
Data: pbmc3k, peripheral blood mononuclear cells from a healthy donor (10x Genomics). The 2,638 cells passing standard QC were used; integer UMI counts were recovered exactly from the cellxgene example copy of the dataset. Analysis: scanpy 1.12 (vst HVGs via flavor="seurat_v3"), leidenalg and umap-learn, random seed 0. Every number and point on this page comes from that run. The droplet animation is a schematic, not data.
